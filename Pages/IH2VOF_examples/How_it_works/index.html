1. The pitch

What this tool is for, and what it is not

Coastal structures are normally designed with hand formulae: van der Meer for rock size, the EurOtop manual for overtopping, Goda for wave forces on walls. Those formulae are built on thousands of laboratory tests and remain the basis of design. Their weakness is that each one assumes a tidy, standard shape. A real section has a berm, a buried wall, a shallow irregular foreshore and three rock layers, and the formulae do not talk to each other.

IH2VOF is a two-dimensional numerical wave tank developed by the Environmental Hydraulics Institute of Cantabria (IHCantabria), Spain. It solves the equations of fluid motion on a grid, tracks the water surface, and lets water flow through rock layers. It can run the actual section from the drawing, with all its details, under an irregular storm.

Use it as

A final, independent check on a detailed section once the hand design is done. Does the model see roughly the overtopping EurOtop predicts? Roughly the force Goda predicts? Does anything unexpected happen at the berm, the toe or the wall?

It is strong at

Comparing two versions of the same section under identical waves, showing mechanisms on video, and catching details the formulae cannot represent.

It is not

A replacement for the design formulae, a physical model test, or a source of design numbers on its own. It is two-dimensional, the rock parameters here are not calibrated, and each run covers only about 45 waves.

The rule proposed for using it: hand methods set the design. The wave tank is run afterwards. Agreement within a factor of about two to three on overtopping, and the same order of magnitude on forces, is confirmation. A larger disagreement is not a new answer; it is a prompt to find out which assumption differs.

2. The workflow

Seven stages, each with a human check

Every stage lives in its own numbered folder under C:\IH2VOF\Western head\ with a copy of the script that produced it, so any number can be traced back. Scripts and repetitive clicking were automated; the green line under each stage is the check a person has to make.

  1. Inputs (00_inputs)Tender drawings, the armour design spreadsheet, the spectral wave model results (MIKE 21 Spectral Waves), the revetment footprint shapefile and the 1 m survey elevation model.Check: are these the current issue of the drawings and the same datum (CGVD2013) everywhere?
  2. Sections from the drawings (01_sections)The section linework was read from the drawing file as vectors and scaled from two known lines on each section (higher high water at +0.76 m and lower low water at −1.5 m). Station positions were fixed by matching the plan to the footprint shapefile, then confirmed against the survey model.Check: overlay the traced section on the drawing (the video does this) and compare the drawn ground with the survey. Here the fit is 0.22 m on the main revetment.
  3. Model geometry (02_geometry)Each layer becomes a polygon with a material name: armour, filter, bedding, backfill, wall, ground. Gauge positions, the pressure zone and the overtopping basin are set here.Check: layer thicknesses and levels against the typical section (crest +5.6 m, berm +2.7 m, toe −1.5 m).
  4. Waves and sea bed offshore (03_forcing)The drawing stops 30 m seaward of the wall. The sea bed from there to the wave paddle comes from the survey model; the wave height and period at the paddle come from the spectral wave model.Check: is the paddle wave physically possible in that depth, and is the water level the one the design uses?
  5. Case files and preparation (04_cases)Geometry and settings are written to the model's input format. The mesh, wave series and paddle motion are generated in the IH2VOF program itself. A script then compares every saved setting with the intended value.Check: the input check must pass; no "floating" water cells trapped under rock.
  6. Run and monitor (05_run)A runner starts the solver, logs progress every two minutes, and post-processes each finished case.Check: the health checks in section 7.
  7. Results and video (06_analysis, 07_hq_videos)Wave heights at the gauges, overtopping, wall force, and the standard video with the drawing overlaid.Check: compare with EurOtop and Goda by hand (section 9) before believing anything.

4. The grid

How many cells, and is that enough?

The tank is divided into rectangular cells 0.5 m long and 0.15 m high: 706 columns by 97 rows, about 68,500 cells. Every result is limited by this resolution, so it is worth knowing how many cells cover each feature.

FeatureSizeCells across itComment

Common guidance for this family of models is at least 10 cells per wave height (preferably 15 to 20), at least 70 to 100 cells per wavelength, and a cell length no more than about 2.5 to 5 times its height. This grid meets those for the waves. The weak spots are horizontal: the wall is under two cells wide and each rock layer is only a few cells thick measured along the slope.

Resolution zones: was the grid made finer near the structure?

No. These runs use one uniform zone: the same 0.5 m by 0.15 m cell from the paddle to the basin and from the floor to the lid. The solid brackets below show that single zone. The dashed brackets show where a finer zone would normally be placed and was not.

Resolution zones for station 0+120. Solid brackets: what was run. Dashed red brackets: the refinement that was not applied.

Why it matters and what to say. The mesh generator in IH2VOF allows the length and the height to be split into sub-zones with smaller cells near the structure, growing gradually offshore. That was not used here, to keep the set-up identical across all stations. The consequence is that the waves are well resolved (about 23 rows per wave height and over 250 columns per wavelength at the paddle) but the structure is coarsely resolved: the wall is under two columns wide and a thin overtopping sheet of 0.1 to 0.2 m is only about one row deep. The honest statement is: "uniform grid, adequate for wave transformation, coarse at the wall and crest; a refined zone near the structure and a repeat run to show the answer does not change is the first improvement before relying on small overtopping values."

5. Rock as numbers

How armour, filter, bedding and wall are represented

The model does not contain individual stones. A rock layer is a zone where water can flow, but feels a drag that depends on how fast it moves and on the stone size. Four numbers describe each layer: the stone size D50, the porosity n (fraction of voids), and two drag coefficients, α and β.

I = a·u + b·u|u| + c·∂u/∂t I is the hydraulic gradient (the push needed to drive the flow), u is the average velocity through the layer. The first term is slow, viscous seepage; the second is turbulent drag, which dominates in rock; the third is the extra inertia of water accelerating around stones.
a = α · (1−n)² / n³ · ν / (g·D50²)     b = β · (1 + 7.5/KC) · (1−n) / n³ · 1 / (g·D50)     c = [1 + γ(1−n)/n] / (n·g), γ = 0.34 ν is the viscosity of water (about 1×10⁻⁶ m²/s), g is gravity, KC is the Keulegan-Carpenter number (how far the water moves back and forth compared with the stone size). This is the form introduced by van Gent (1995) and used in the COBRAS and IH2VOF family (Liu et al., 1999; Hsu et al., 2002).

The key point for a reviewer: α and β were kept the same for every rock layer (α = 200, β = 1.1, the values of Liu et al., 1999). What changes from layer to layer is D50 and n. Because a goes with 1/D50² and b with 1/D50, small stone resists flow far more than large stone, with no change to the coefficients.

Try the numbers yourself

Nominal diameter from mass: Dn50 = (M50 / ρ)^(1/3) with rock density ρ = 2650 kg/m³. For 7,800 kg this gives 1.43 m, the value on the design sheet.

What to say when asked "why those values?"

  • Published ranges. van Gent (1995) fitted α = 1000 and β = 1.1 to oscillating-flow tests. Liu et al. (1999) used α = 200 and β = 1.1. Work with IH2VOF commonly uses α = 200 with β between 0.8 and 1.1. Jensen et al. (2014) recommend α = 500 and β = 2.0 for a different formulation. There is no single agreed pair.
  • Which one matters. In armour-sized rock the turbulent term b·u² is thousands of times larger than the viscous term, so β matters and α hardly does. Castiglione et al. (2023) found that changing β by 10 to 20 % changed computed overtopping of a rubble mound by more than 150 %.
  • Consequence. The coefficients here are literature values, not calibrated to tests on this structure. Overtopping from this model carries that uncertainty. A sensitivity run with β = 0.8 and β = 1.5 is the honest way to show the range.
  • Stone size used. The model's D50 was set equal to the nominal diameter Dn50 from the drawing. Strictly Dn50 is about 0.84 × D50 for quarry rock, so the layers are modelled as slightly finer, and slightly more resistant, than a strict conversion would give.
There is no "core" in this model, and the ground is watertight. This is a revetment laid on existing ground, not a breakwater with a quarry-run core. Below the bedding layer the ground is a solid, impermeable boundary. Water cannot drain into the beach, which tends to hold water levels inside the rock slightly high. The wall is also a solid, impermeable block. The grain sizes for bedding (0.05 m) and backfill (0.05 m) are assumed and still to be confirmed against the specification.

Three honest caveats on the waves.
  • The wave model was run at a water level of +1.46 m, without the surge. The design spreadsheet pairs those waves with +2.38 m. Near the structure the waves are depth-limited, so the true wave at +2.38 m is probably larger than the 3.24 m used to size the armour. This is a question for the design, not just the wave tank.
  • The paddle wave (3.5 m) is smaller than the wave model has at that point (4.67 m). The tank relies on the foreshore to control what reaches the structure, which is reasonable when waves are depth-limited but should be shown, not assumed: compare the tank wave height where the bed is −2.5 m with the design value.
  • About 47 waves is far short of the 500 to 1000 recommended for stable overtopping statistics. The largest wave group arrives at about 370 s; a 300 s run would miss it.

9. Hand checks

Confirming against EurOtop and Goda

Overtopping: EurOtop (2018), rubble mound slope

q / √(g·Hm0³) = 0.09 · exp[ −( 1.5 · Rc / (Hm0 · γf · γβ) )^1.3 ]    then × Cr,   Cr = 3.06 · exp(−1.5 · Gc / Hm0), at most 1 Mean-value form for steep rough slopes. Rc is the crest height above still water, Hm0 the wave height at the toe, γf the roughness factor (0.40 for two layers of rock on a permeable core, 0.55 on an impermeable core), γβ the wave angle factor (1 for head-on waves), Gc the crest width. Check the equation numbers and factors in the manual before quoting.

With the design wave of 3.24 m, freeboard 3.22 m (crest +5.6 m, water +2.38 m) and the 5.8 m crest, EurOtop gives of the order of 1 to 10 litres per second per metre depending on the roughness factor. Put in the wave height the tank actually had at the toe gauge (2.29 m) and the answer drops to a few hundredths of a litre per second per metre, effectively zero over 47 waves. So the tank's "zero" is consistent with EurOtop for the wave the tank delivered. The open question is which toe wave height is right, which is the same question raised in section 6. That is the value of the cross-check: it points at the input that needs settling.

Wave force on walls: how Goda's method works

Yoshimi Goda (1974; textbook 2000) gave a single set of formulae for the wave pressure on a vertical wall that works for both non-breaking and breaking waves. It is the standard for caisson breakwaters and quay walls worldwide. The idea is simple: assume a pressure diagram of a fixed shape, then use coefficients to set its size.

Goda's pressure diagram. h: water depth in front of the wall. d: depth to the top of the armour on the mound. h′: depth to the base of the wall. hc: wall crest above still water. B: base width.

  1. Choose the design wave: the largest single wave, not the significant waveHmax = 1.8 × Hs when the wall is seaward of the breaker zone. Inside the breaker zone, Hmax is the largest breaking wave at a point 5 × Hs seaward of the wall (depth hb there). The period is the significant wave period, roughly Tp ÷ 1.1. This is the most common mistake: putting Hs into the formula under-predicts by almost half.
  2. How high the pressure reachesη* = 0.75 (1 + cos β) Hmax, where β is the wave angle to the wall normal (0 for head-on; Goda allows reducing the real angle by 15 degrees as a safety margin). For head-on waves the pressure reaches 1.5 × Hmax above still water.
  3. Three coefficients set the sizeα1 = 0.6 + ½ [ (4πh/L) ÷ sinh(4πh/L) ]². The effect of period: 1.1 for long waves in shallow water, 0.6 for short waves in deep water.
    α2 = the smaller of [ (hb − d) ÷ (3 hb) ] (Hmax/d)² and 2d/Hmax. The extra pressure when a high mound makes the wave break onto the wall.
    α3 = 1 − (h′/h) [ 1 − 1 ÷ cosh(2πh/L) ]. How fast pressure decays with depth.
    L is the wavelength in depth h.
  4. The pressuresp1 = ½ (1 + cos β)(α1 + α2 cos²β) ρ g Hmax at still water level.
    p3 = α3 p1 at the wall base. p4 = p1 (1 − hc/η*) at the crest if the wall is lower than η*, otherwise zero.
    pu = ½ (1 + cos β) α1 α3 ρ g Hmax, the uplift at the seaward edge of the base, falling to zero at the landward edge.
  5. Add up the areasHorizontal force per metre of wall = ½ (p1 + p3) h′ + ½ (p1 + p4) hc*, where hc* is the smaller of hc and η*. Uplift = ½ pu B. The wall is then checked for sliding and overturning with safety factors of at least 1.2.

Try Goda yourself

The starting values are an illustrative caisson, not a project structure. Sea water density 1,030 kg/m³.

What Goda is good for

Sliding and overturning design of vertical walls standing in water, under non-breaking and ordinary breaking waves. Calibrated on model tests and on real breakwaters that slid or stayed put.

Where it under-predicts

Violent "impulsive" breaking on a high mound or steep bed. Takahashi et al. (1994) added an impulsive coefficient that replaces α2 when larger; in the Dominica check it nearly doubled the force.

Where it does not apply

Walls whose base is above the water, walls set back behind a rock crest or beach, and local slamming pressures on small elements. It gives a force for stability, not a pressure map for concrete design.

Goda against the wave tank

Goda's method gives the pressure on a vertical wall standing in water. It was applied to the two Portsmouth docks in the Dominica study, where the walls do stand in water at the higher sea level:

The model gave a little under half of Goda in both cases. That is the expected direction: Goda is a design envelope, and a model with 0.5 m cells and smoothed pressures cannot resolve the sharpest part of an impact. The conclusion drawn was that Goda governs structural design and the model forces are a lower bound.

At Western Head, Goda does not strictly apply. The wall base is at about +2.6 m, above the still water level, and in the proposed design the wall is buried behind the rock. The 11.9 kN/m is pressure transmitted through the rock, not a wave striking a wall in water. The right hand check there is a bore-impact or post-overtopping load estimate for walls set back on a crest (see EurOtop 2018 on storm walls, and Goda's own limits of application). Saying this, instead of forcing a Goda number, shows understanding of the method.

10. Reliability and validation

Why this model family can be trusted, and how far

IH2VOF descends from COBRAS, developed at Cornell University (Lin and Liu, 1998), which was extended at the University of Cantabria (as COBRAS-UC) and then rewritten as IH2VOF. It solves the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations with a volume-of-fluid surface, and volume-averaged equations inside porous layers. It has been compared with laboratory measurements for over twenty years.

StudyWhat was compared with laboratory data
Lin and Liu (1998)Breaking waves in the surf zone: surface elevation, velocities, turbulence.
Liu et al. (1999); Hsu et al. (2002)Flow through porous structures and in front of a composite breakwater; the porous-flow equations used here.
Garcia et al. (2004); Losada et al. (2005); Lara et al. (2006)Regular and random waves on low-crested permeable breakwaters: surface elevation, pressure and velocity inside and outside the rock.
Losada et al. (2008)Overtopping of rubble mound breakwaters: instantaneous and mean discharge against flume tests.
Lara et al. (2008)Low-mound breakwaters with a crown wall: free surface and pressures.

Torres-Freyermuth et al. (2010); Lara et al. (2011)Short and long wave transformation and run-up on beaches.

Well supported

Wave transformation and breaking on a foreshore, water levels and pressures in and around rock structures, and mean overtopping of rubble mounds, when the porous coefficients are calibrated and the turbulence model is on.

Supported with care

Forces on crown walls: good agreement for slowly varying (pulsating) loads; short impact peaks depend strongly on grid and sampling.

Not shown by this study

No comparison with measurements was made for Western Head. The published validation shows the tool can work; it does not validate this particular setup.

Verification done in this project. Geometry checked against the drawing by overlay; station positions against survey; input files against intended settings; delivered wave height against target; water volume conserved; EurOtop consistency for the delivered wave; and, in the Dominica study, forces against Goda and a full set of before-and-after comparisons. An early Dominica batch was found to have vegetation floating above the bed and a closed wall contaminating overtopping; both were caught by these checks and rerun.

11. Limits

Everything a reviewer should be told up front

  • Two-dimensional. One slice, waves head-on. No oblique waves, no flow along the shore, no gaps or roundheads.
  • Turbulence model switched off. These runs used the option without the k-epsilon turbulence closure. Breaking is still captured by the grid, but energy loss in breaking and inside the rock is less well represented than in the published validations, which used the turbulence model.
  • Porous coefficients not calibrated. Literature values; overtopping is sensitive to β.
  • Short storm. About 47 waves. Peak values depend on which waves happened to be in the series; one random series only.
  • Impermeable ground and wall, no settlement, no stone movement. The model cannot show armour damage. Rock stability remains a van der Meer question.
  • Resolution. 0.5 m cells: the wall is under two cells wide, thin overtopping sheets are coarsely resolved, impact pressures are smoothed.
  • Paddle wave capped by depth and taken from a wave model run at a lower water level than the tank.
  • Assumed grain sizes for bedding and backfill.
  • Wind, spray and air entrainment are not modelled.

12. Do it yourself

Reproducing the key numbers by hand from the drawing

  1. Read the section.From the typical section: crest level and width, berm level and width, toe level, slopes, layer thicknesses, wall top and base. Convert a level to model height with y = level − (−5.5) m; the still water level +2.38 m becomes 7.88 m.
  2. Turn stone masses into sizes.Dn50 = (M50 / 2650)^(1/3). Armour 6 to 10 tonnes, M50 about 8 tonnes, gives about 1.45 m. Filter 400 to 1200 kg, M50 about 800 kg, gives 0.67 m.
  3. Count cells.Divide each thickness by 0.15 m vertically and each width by 0.5 m horizontally. Fewer than about five cells across a feature means it is only roughly resolved.
  4. Compute the drag terms.Use the calculator in section 5, or the formulae directly. For armour: a ≈ 5×10⁻⁵ s/m and b ≈ 0.73 s²/m², so at 1 m/s the gradient is about 0.73, almost all from the turbulent term.
  5. Check the paddle wave.Depth 7.88 m; cap 0.45 × 7.88 = 3.55 m; used 3.5 m. Wavelength from the dispersion relation for 15.1 s in 7.88 m is about 130 m, so 260 cells per wavelength and 23 cells per wave height.
  6. Estimate the time step.Courant limit ≈ 0.3 × cell height ÷ fastest vertical velocity, or cell length ÷ fastest horizontal velocity. With velocities of several metres per second, steps of around 0.01 s are expected, as seen.
  7. Predict before you look.Run EurOtop with the design wave and with the delivered toe wave (section 9). Write both down. Then open the result. If the model is far outside that range, find out why before using it.
